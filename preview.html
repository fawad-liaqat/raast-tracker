Analysis

Raast P2P volumes grew 8.7x in three years (147M → 496M → 1.28B). Transaction value grew from PKR 3.1T (FY23) to 11.5T (FY24) to 29.6T (FY25) — a 9.5x value increase over the same period. Total retail digital transactions reached 9.1 billion, with mobile banking apps alone processing 6.2 billion (52% YoY growth). SBP FY25

Critical distinction

The 88% digital share is by transaction volume, not value. By value, the digital share is approximately 29% (Q2 FY25). High-frequency, low-value transactions (P2P transfers, airtime top-ups, utility bills) inflate the volume share. Cash dominates high-value activity: retail commerce, wholesale trade, real estate, informal markets. SBP Q2 FY25

Why deployment alone fails

Terminal deployment measures supply. It does not measure whether merchants prefer digital acceptance over cash. With MDR fees of 1.5-2.5% per transaction and tax documentation requirements, many merchants with QR codes still prefer cash. A QR sticker on the wall does not mean a QR payment at the counter.

Pakistan's 731,814 branchless banking agents primarily facilitate cash-in/cash-out, not digital payments. They serve the cash economy, not the digital one.

Block C — Counter Narrative

The Cash Persistence: Revealed Preference

ATM Cash Withdrawal Rate
98%

20,341 ATMs | 140 transactions per ATM per day SBP FY25

What this reveals

This is the single most telling statistic in Pakistan's payment data. People who already have bank accounts and debit cards, who have already overcome the trust barrier enough to open an account, still convert digital money to physical cash before spending it. This is a revealed preference, not an access problem.

Behavioral diagnosis

For merchants: Accepting digital payments means MDR fees (1.5-2.5%) plus a tax documentation trail. Cash carries zero fee and zero trail. For businesses in the informal economy (estimated 40% of GDP), this is rational avoidance.

For consumers: Digital payment acceptance remains inconsistent at point of sale. Attempting digital payment and encountering a cash-preferring merchant creates social friction. The path of least resistance is cash.

Trust data: Only 9% of excluded adults trust banks. Only 8% trust mobile money providers. Just 36% of all adults are comfortable using any financial service. K-FIS 2024

What the average hides

52.9 annual transactions per account (~1 per week) sounds reasonable. But this is an average across all registered users, including dormant accounts. An estimated 54 million bank deposit accounts hold balances below PKR 5,000. A small fraction of active accounts likely drives the majority of Raast volume, while millions of registered accounts never transact digitally.

The gap is not between infrastructure and the average user. It is between infrastructure and the majority of registered accounts that remain inactive.

Part 2: Why the Gap Exists
Three-Layer Framework

The Behavioral Last Mile: Three Compounding Barriers

The gap between 88% digital volume share and a cash-dominant economy is not an infrastructure problem. It is a demand-side behavioral problem with three compounding layers. Each barrier reinforces the others: a person who distrusts banks, has no incentive to switch from cash, and cannot navigate a digital interface faces all three simultaneously.

1
Trust Deficit
Core finding: People reject digital payments because they distrust the institutions, regardless of whether the infrastructure exists.
Evidence: Only 9% of excluded adults trust banks. Only 8% trust mobile money providers. Just 36% of all adults report comfort using any financial service. 85% of excluded adults rely on family and friends for credit—not because formal options are unavailable, but because the informal system operates on trust relationships that institutions cannot replicate. K-FIS 2024
Mechanism: Cash offers anonymity and immediacy. For the estimated 40% of GDP operating informally, digital transactions create a record trail perceived as tax exposure. This is rational, not irrational.
Comparative: Kenya's M-Pesa overcame distrust through agent networks—local shopkeepers embedded in community trust. Users trusted the person, not the institution. Pakistan's Raast is app-based and institution-facing, bypassing this intermediary trust layer entirely.
2
Incentive Misalignment
Core finding: The incentive structure actively favors cash even when digital alternatives exist.
Evidence: The 98% ATM cash withdrawal rate is revealed preference: people who already have accounts choose to convert digital money to physical cash. Merchants face MDR fees of 1.5-2.5%. Cash carries zero fee. BISP/Ehsaas G2P payments have high adoption because the incentive structure is forced, not voluntary. SBP FY25
Mechanism: Merchants face two costs from digital acceptance: MDR and tax documentation. Consumers face inconsistent acceptance at point of sale. Both parties find cash the path of least resistance.
Comparative: India mandated zero MDR on UPI below Rs 2,000 (2019). This removed the merchant penalty entirely. UPI grew from 8.7B to 228.3B transactions in six years. Pakistan has not implemented equivalent relief.
3
Literacy Friction
Core finding: Even willing adopters face friction from interfaces designed for digitally literate users.
Evidence: K-FIS 2024 shows a significant urban-rural gap in DFS usage even at equal access levels. Phone ownership: 82% men vs 46% women. Account ownership: 56% men vs 14% women. The gap is not only about infrastructure—it is a gender equity problem mediated through technology. K-FIS 2024
Mechanism: Banking app Urdu interfaces are poor translations. USSD menus require multi-step memorization. Biometrics fail for manual laborers. Raast is smartphone-dependent—feature phone, Urdu-primary, and low-literacy populations are excluded by design, not by infrastructure absence.
Comparative: India's BHIM app launched in 13 languages. UPI 123PAY (2022) enabled feature phone transactions via IVR. Pakistan's Raast has not implemented equivalent accessibility.

Key takeaway

Neither India nor Kenya achieved mass adoption through infrastructure alone. India's UPI succeeded because policy design deliberately addressed each barrier: zero MDR (incentive), Jan Dhan (access), BHIM (literacy), demonetization (behavioral shock). Kenya's M-Pesa succeeded because it solved a specific acute pain point (remittances) through a trust mechanism (agents) that did not require institutional credibility. Pakistan has not yet found its equivalent.

Evidence-Based Interventions

Five Actions That Close the Behavioral Gap

Each recommendation targets a specific layer, specifies who implements it, describes the mechanism, projects the expected effect, and cites the international precedent.

1
Eliminate MDR on transactions below PKR 5,000
SBPNPCI UPI Statistics

Mechanism: Regulatory mandate removing merchant fees on small digital transactions covering most retail purchases.

Effect: Removes the direct cost penalty making cash preferable. Accelerates merchant acceptance of Raast/QR.

Precedent: India's zero MDR on UPI below Rs 2,000 (2019). UPI grew 26x from 8.7B to 228.3B transactions.

2
Build feature phone Raast access via USSD/IVR
SBP + BanksUPI 123PAY

Mechanism: USSD/IVR-based Raast channels requiring no smartphone or internet.

Effect: Extends access to the 54% of women without phones (via shared feature phones) and rural populations.

Precedent: India's UPI 123PAY (2022) enables feature phone UPI via missed call, IVR, proximity NFC.

3
Restructure agent commissions for digital facilitation
SBP + BB ProvidersCBK Annual Reports

Mechanism: Redesign commissions to reward facilitating Raast P2P and merchant payments over cash cycling.

Effect: Converts 731,814 agents from cash access points into digital payment facilitators embedded in community trust.

Precedent: Kenya's M-Pesa agent commission model aligned agent economics with adoption growth.

4
Tax incentive for documented digital spending
FBRSouth Korea Card Tax Program

Mechanism: 0.5-1% tax rebate on documented digital retail transactions.

Effect: Shifts consumer incentive from "digital = tax visibility risk" to "digital = tax saving."

Precedent: South Korea's tax deduction for card payments accelerated cash-to-card transition in the early 2000s.

5
Mandate Urdu-first financial app design standards
SBPBHIM UPI

Mechanism: Binding guidelines: native Urdu interfaces, max 4-step flows, voice-guided options.

Effect: Reduces literacy friction for ~70% of population whose primary language is not English.

Precedent: India's BHIM app design standards for regional languages materially reduced literacy barriers.

The core lesson

Infrastructure is necessary. It is not sufficient. India and Kenya did not achieve mass adoption because they built better technology. They achieved it because they deliberately designed incentive structures, trust mechanisms, and accessible interfaces that converted infrastructure into behavior. Pakistan's Raast is architecturally sound but behaviorally incomplete.

Known Limitations

  • No district-level Raast data. Urban/rural POS density used as proxy.
  • Unique transacting accounts not reported. Total registered accounts used.
  • 88% digital share is by volume, not value. Value share ~29% (Q2 FY25).
  • User categories contain overlap (mobile banking, BB app, e-wallet).
  • Kenya early-stage data is narrative, not structured. Direct volume comparison not possible.
  • Adoption Gap Index is computed, not SBP-reported.

Theoretical Framework

  • Technology Acceptance Model (TAM): Davis (1989). Trust deficit maps to perceived usefulness and ease of use.
  • Diffusion of Innovations: Rogers (2003). Pakistan at the chasm between early adopters and early majority.
  • Prospect Theory: Kahneman & Tversky (1979). Loss aversion explains why perceived digital risk outweighs benefit.
  • Institutional Trust: Zucker (1986). Low institutional trust explains why agent models work where bank models fail.
